Good Faith Estimate

Under the federal No Surprises Act, patients who are uninsured or who choose not to use insurance have the right to a written estimate of expected charges before non-emergency care. This page explains how that works at our clinic.

What follows is a draft outline, not a notice in force.

Nothing below is binding. It is a plain-language summary of what each section of the finished notice will cover, published so you can see how estimates are handled while the reviewed version is prepared.

01What a Good Faith Estimate is

A written list of the care we expect to provide and what we expect to charge for it, given to you before that care begins.

02Who has the right to one

Patients who do not have insurance, and patients who have insurance but choose not to use it for this care. If you are using insurance, the estimate rules that apply to you are different, and this section will say which ones.

03When you will get one

On request, and in advance of scheduled non-emergency care.

04What the estimate includes

The examinations, imaging, nerve and muscle testing and treatment visits we expect, itemised, with the expected charge for each.

05Why a final bill can differ from the estimate

An estimate is a forecast made before you are examined. Findings change a plan. If your care changes, the charges change, and this section will explain how you are told about that.

06Auto-accident claims, liens and third-party billing

If your care is being billed to a claim or held on a lien, this section will explain how an estimate interacts with that arrangement, and what you owe if the claim does not resolve as expected. Nothing here is a promise that a claim, an insurer or a settlement will cover any amount.

07If your bill is higher than your estimate

Federal law gives patients a dispute route when a bill exceeds the estimate by more than a set amount, within a set time.

08How to request an estimate

Ask at the front desk, or call. You can ask before you book, and you can ask for it in writing.
